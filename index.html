Introduction to Lean Manufacturing Before exploring the many tools and techniques used in Lean Manufacturing, it is important to begin with a clear understanding of what the concept of Lean really means. In my early years in the industry, it was not clear to me the meaning of Lean Manufacturing. The translation to Spanish, my native language, the term was converted to “Manufactura Esbelta” which did not make real sense to me. It was at that time when I decided to investigate the definition of Lean by simply looking at the dictionary. Besides the relationship of the term as synonym of inclination, I was able to find a piece of text making reference to the concept of meat. This definition actually became one of my favorite ways to explain the common connotation of Lean. As an example, when we go to the meat market and order a portion of lean meat, we mean to order a piece of meat that is expected to be clean and free from fat, skin, nerves and bones, in other words, no waste. We are asking only for what adds value: pure meat. In a restaurant, ordering a filet mignon is a good example of the same thing. These two instances capture the essence of Lean: remove what is not edible (valuable) and preserve only what is safe to eat, healthy, and most important tasty. I can always understand the idea of having a “lean” piece of steak, but I can hardly digest the meaning of a “svelte” piece of steak, I hope this makes sense. Important note: The word “Lean” does not appear on Toyota Corporation’s website, nor is it used in Taiichi Ohno’s book The Toyota Production System, first released in 1978 and later translated to English until 1988. In that sense, Lean is by far a Western interpretation of the principles behind the Toyota Production System.   2. Lean in the Context of the Human Body To explain Lean in simple terms, I often use ordinary examples such as the human body, our homes and, of course, the manufacturing plant. The human body offers a particularly useful comparison. In general, a lean body suggests a healthy proportion between weight and height, with no visible excess. The medical community often uses BMI, or Body Mass Index, as a basic reference for this relationship. BMI = Weight / Height² = kg / m² For example, a person weighing 80 kg and measuring 1.80 m tall has a BMI of approximately 24.7. On the BMI scale, this falls within the healthy-weight range: Consideration Index Value Underweight Below 18.5 Healthy weight 18.5 to 24.9 Overweight 25.0 to 29.9 Obesity Above 30.0 Note: BMI is only a reference. It does not distinguish between fat, muscle, or bone structure, and it does not account for age, sex, or the distribution of weight. From this perspective, the general perception of Lean is associated with being slim, svelte, healthy, agile, and energetic. Applied to work, Lean suggests a system with no work overload, no excess activity, and no hidden waste.   3. Lean at Home Another simple example comes from the home. Whenever I clean my garage or home office, my wife takes a deep breath and says, “I love it. It looks wonderful, organized, minimalist, and lean.” That reaction captures an important idea: Lean is often visible. We can recognize it when a space looks organized, uncluttered, and little or no waste. At its most basic level, Lean refers to something clean, slim, organized, and free from excess or waste. Modern preferences for minimalist or “feng shui” environments reflect this same idea. Keep this simple interpretation in mind, because later we will apply it directly to the production workplace.  4. Lean in the Manufacturing Plant In a manufacturing environment, Lean can be perceived through visual recognition, workplace organization, and workers discipline. A lean plant typically shows the following characteristics: - Work areas are clean, organized, and uncluttered. - Workplace management practices are visible and easy to see. - Housekeeping standards are strong, with little or no visible waste. - Safety is clearly prioritized, and quality is a given, productivity is high. - People are treated with respect as they focus on work with the right attitude. - Visitors experience a “wow” moment because of flow, order and discipline. However, Lean is more than perception, workplace organization, or a clean facility. Those are visible signs, but they are not the whole system. At a deeper level, Lean is: - A discipline, mindset, philosophy, and culture. Top management leads. - A habit and consistent behavior. All people are fully engaged. - The daily work is a reflection of company values and leadership. - A workplace environment that shines because of these practices. - A structured system that shows Lean is important and actively followed. - It is al about corporate’s passion, obsession, and commitment.   EXERCISE Objective: Measure the Lean Readiness Level of the Plant using a Radar Chart as a self-assessment tool.
